## History notes (flagged, not changed; from memory, not yet checked against sources)
1. **960 AD.** The Song dynasty was founded in early 960, so the court in this story would be only weeks or months old, with a small harem. The story fits a later reign much better. The closest real case is 1010: Consort Liu, who had no son, took the newborn of a palace maid, Lady Li, and raised him as her own; he became Emperor Renzong. Suggest "It is 1010 AD" or no year.
2. **"Legally forbidden" foods.** Rules for wet nurses (no pungent foods, no anger or grief because it "spoils the milk") come from medical writing such as Sun Simiao's Tang-era *Qianjin Fang*, and from household practice, not from law. Suggest "forbidden" without "legally".
3. **The bowl of milk.** A wet nurse feeds the baby at her breast, so a poisoner would target her food, not a bowl of milk; and the concubine "feeding him" from the bowl contradicts why a nurse was hired. If you want it airtight: the bowl could be the nurse's own meal (the fish soup) that she must eat before nursing, or milk she expressed for the prince.
4. **"Third concubine."** Song consorts had ranked titles rather than numbers. Fine as storytelling.
5. **Recruitment.** Eunuchs scouring the provinces is dramatized; imperial wet nurses were usually found through officials among commoner women near the capital. Some imperial wet nurses were later rewarded with honorary titles, which could make a strong twist or footnote.
6. **Arsenic.** Arsenic (pishuang) was a well-known poison in Song China and is close to tasteless, so that line holds up.
7. **Length.** 754 words is about 5.5 minutes: above Facebook's 3-minute in-stream ad minimum, below the 8-12 minute target.

## Anna's script (as written)

It is 960 AD. You haven't slept in three days. Your head is pounding, your breasts are engorged, and your hands are shaking so violently you're afraid you'll drop the porcelain bowl in front of you.
Why? Because the Emperor's third concubine is convinced the Empress is trying to murder her newborn son. And in the Inner Court of the Song Dynasty, a jealous wife doesn't target the baby directly. She targets the milk.
Which means before a single drop touches the lips of the infant prince... you have to taste-test it first. You take a breath, staring down at the white liquid. If the Empress succeeded, this is your last meal. How did you find yourself in the most dangerous room in the empire? Just three months ago, you were a peasant woman in a muddy village. You had just given birth to your own child. But in the Imperial Court, a woman's milk is a commodity.
When the Emperor's favorite concubine fell pregnant, her eunuchs scoured the provinces looking for women with the perfect "constitution." They inspected your skin, your teeth, and the health of your own baby. You were chosen.
But there was a price. To feed a prince, you had to leave your own child behind in the mud. You were brought through the massive vermilion gates of the palace, stripped, scrubbed, and dressed in silks you aren't even allowed to keep.
You quickly learned the brutal hierarchy of the Harem. The Lady who hired you is a consort—a glorified second-class wife. Her entire existence, her safety, and her wealth depend on the survival of the boy she just birthed. If he lives, she might become an Empress Dowager one day. If he dies, she is nothing. And you? You are just the vessel keeping her ambition alive. You thought living in a palace meant luxury, but you quickly realize you are a prisoner of your own biology. The court physicians watch you like a hawk.
You are legally forbidden from eating garlic, onions, or heavy spices. They believe "hot" foods will sour your milk and corrupt the royal bloodline. Every day, you are forced to eat bland, unseasoned pork fat and fish soup to keep your milk rich.
You are forbidden from showing anger or grief. The doctors believe if you cry, your milk will curdle with poison and give the young master colic. If the baby gets a fever, it's not bad luck—it's your fault. You will be beaten.
But the physical restrictions are nothing compared to the psychological warfare. Look out the lattice window of the nursery. See those women walking in the courtyard? That is the Empress and her entourage. She has no sons. Her womb is empty, and her biological clock is ticking against her. Every time she looks toward your nursery, your stomach twists into knots. Back to the bowl in your hands. The concubine is hovering over you, her eyes wild with postpartum paranoia. She swears she saw a kitchen eunuch whispering to one of the Empress's maids near the well this morning.
"Drink," she whispers to you. Her voice is trembling, but her hand is on the hilt of a guard's dagger.
You bring the cool porcelain to your lips. You know that if the Empress did bribe the kitchen staff, they likely used arsenic or crushed lead. It won't taste like anything. You swallow.
One second passes. Two. Your heart thumps against your ribs. You wait for the burning in your throat. You wait for your stomach to cramp.
Nothing happens. Your lungs expand as you take a shaky breath. It's safe. For today.
The concubine snatches the bowl from your hands, her face instantly shifting from terror to cold, aristocratic indifference. She cradles her son and feeds him, completely forgetting you are even in the room. You step back into the shadows of the silk curtains, wiping your mouth. You survived the morning. But the prince has to eat again in four hours. This was the hidden reality of the women who built dynasties. History books remember the Emperors, the grand battles, and the beautiful Empresses. But the foundations of these empires were built on the backs of forgotten peasant women. Women like you, who traded their own children's lives for a chance to survive in a golden cage, risking death with every single feeding.
When we look at the glitz and glamour of ancient empires, we have to ask ourselves: who paid the price for it?
